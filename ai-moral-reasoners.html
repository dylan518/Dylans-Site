AI as Moral Reasoners

Nov 2, 2026 · Dylan Wilson

Ask an AI a science question and we assume it’s reasoning well over what’s known. We expect it to struggle at the uncertain frontier, and to get more accurate as models get stronger. We should apply the same logic to morality. Science weighs all the evidence to find the most probable model of the world. Ethics does the same: it weighs everyone’s moral judgments, our observations of right and wrong, to find the model that best explains all of them.

In the 1850s, most doctors believed diseases like cholera spread through bad air. Then the evidence came in: John Snow traced a London cholera outbreak to a single water pump, and microscopes revealed microbes in the sick. The reasoning followed: one explanation, germs, fit all of it. Science changed its mind, and no one doubts it today.

Ethics works in much the same way. For centuries, many thinkers treated animals as machines that didn’t really feel pain. Then the evidence came in: Darwin showed we share ancestry with them, and neuroscience found they have the same pain systems we do. The reasoning followed: if pain matters in humans, consistency says it matters in any creature that feels it. The moral view changed, and animal cruelty is now illegal in much of the world.

Moral evidence doesn’t have to come from a lab. Many Americans once accepted slavery. Frederick Douglass’s Narrative (1845) gave them new evidence, showing in his own words what slavery did to the people living under it. The reasoning followed: principles they already held, like the Golden Rule, couldn’t be squared with what they now saw. The moral view changed.

In each case, we took the evidence and found the explanation that fit it best. Moral evidence is sparser and messier than a microscope slide, so the reasoning is harder, but it’s the same kind of problem. We already trust AI to reason this way in biology, physics, and math, and it keeps improving as models get stronger. If we expect AI to one day reach a near-perfect understanding of biology, we should expect the same for ethics: the most accurate explanation of right and wrong that the evidence allows.

Philosophers have a name for the reasoner who always finds that best explanation: the ideal observer, who knows the facts, never contradicts itself, and weighs everyone. It was always a thought experiment. In theory, an AI reasoning optimally over everyone’s moral judgments would be one. Today’s models are already getting close: like a good reasoner, they pick the commonsense answer on clear moral cases and are uncertain on hard ones (Scherrer et al., 2023). Future systems could become optimal reasoners: an ideal observer we can actually build.
