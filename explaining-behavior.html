Explaining Behavior in Dylan

In Nepal, a man struck up a conversation with me as if by chance. He was a student who wanted to practice his English, and he happened to have the day off. He spent the day showing me around, calling me “my friend.” At the end, he asked me to buy his paintings, saying his whole family had died in the recent earthquake. The details didn’t add up, and my gut told me confidently that he was lying. Later, I saw him running the same routine on another tourist, and locals knew him as a scammer. Still, he clearly wasn’t rich, and $20 might have helped him. I’m not sure what the right call was. But I know which choice was safe: giving him the money. Not because it did the most good, but because no one would ever blame me for it.

The safe choice and the good choice aren’t the same, but they’re easy to confuse. Norms like generosity, politeness, and doing what’s expected are useful defaults, and most of the time they point the right way. But part of their appeal is that they protect you. Follow them and no one can blame you, even when the outcome is bad. Break them and you own the result, even when you were right. That asymmetry makes the safe choice feel like the moral one. And if we flinch at a risk that small, we’ll never take the ones that matter.

People once ran at a wall of spears for glory and Valhalla. Now we won’t risk $10k of salary, a one-in-a-million chance of dying, or looking strange to a stranger. Something great is worth far worse odds. We’re so afraid of being judged that we’re pulling away from each other. This isn’t prudence. It’s shrinking, and we call it wisdom.

Staying in the trees would have kept us alive longer. We came down anyway, and everything worth anything came after: cathedrals, symphonies, the moon. None of it was safe or necessary for survival. Survival is the floor, not the goal. With AI, we mostly ask how to contain it, not how far it could carry us. I can’t fully justify this rationally, but I believe there’s value in doing incredible things. I hope this makes you step back and see how small our fears are compared to what humanity can accomplish and build.
